Foresters have always had to decide today what a stand will look like in eighty years. For most of the history of silviculture, experience was a good enough guide: the climate those trees would grow in resembled the one their predecessors had known. That is no longer true, and it changes what students need to learn.

My teaching sits on that seam. I start from traditional silviculture — stand structure, competition, tree selection, the forester's eye in the field — and bring in dynamic models as one more instrument in the forester's toolbox, not a replacement for it. Students leave able to do both: read a stand, and question what a model says about its future.

## How I teach

<div class="cards">
  <div class="card">
    <h3>See the stand</h3>
    <p>With the Stand Visualization System (SVS), inventory tables become three-dimensional
    stands. Students draw the same forest before and after an intervention and learn to read
    layering, crown space and gaps — before a single simulation runs.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="card">
    <h3>Mark the trees</h3>
    <p>Marteloscope exercises put students in the forester's boots: which trees stay, which go,
    and why. Their selection decisions are then translated into explicit rules a model can
    follow.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="card">
    <h3>Let time pass</h3>
    <p>The marked stand is grown decades ahead with a forest gap model, comparing growth,
    mortality, regeneration, timber, carbon and risk across silvicultural strategies and
    climates.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="card">
    <h3>Argue with the model</h3>
    <p>When projections and field intuition disagree, that is the lesson. Students learn when to
    trust a model, when to challenge it, and how to explain the difference to a practitioner.</p>
  </div>
</div>
